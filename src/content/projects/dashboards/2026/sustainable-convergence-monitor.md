<p>A fixed-position monitoring console for Sustainable Convergence, one of the six H Heuristics concepts: closing the gap between developing and high-income economies without the damage of past growth. A world map with a 2000–2024 timeline shows 15 indicators in four groups: convergence, basic services, human capital and environment. Select any economy, region or income group for its index score, catch-up momentum, convergence trajectory to 2100, the number of people without each basic service, and a Development Multiplier note on its weakest service.</p>

<p>Three analysis views sit under the map. The <b>convergence test</b> plots starting income against subsequent growth and fits a β-convergence slope. <b>Regional catch-up</b> tracks low- and middle-income economies, East Asia, South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa as a share of high-income GDP per capita. The <b>data table</b> ranks and sorts every economy.</p>

<h2 id="method">Method and sources</h2>

<p><b>Data.</b> World Bank World Development Indicators (API, updated 11 November 2026): GDP per capita PPP (constant 2021 $), access to electricity, clean cooking, basic drinking water, basic sanitation, internet use, life expectancy, under-5 mortality, CO₂ per capita, PM2.5 exposure, renewable energy share and population. Where the World Bank does not publish a group aggregate, the dashboard uses population-weighted means of members with at least 80% population coverage.</p>

<p><b>Sustainable Convergence Index (illustrative composite).</b> Development level (45%), which averages eight scaled measures of income, health and basic-service access. Convergence momentum (30%), which is 10-year GDP-per-capita growth minus that of the high-income group. Footprint (25%), which combines carbon intensity of GDP and PM2.5 exposure. The weights are judgemental and are offered as a proposed framework open to testing. GDP-based scores overstate economies where multinational profit accounting inflates GDP, such as Ireland.</p>

<p><b>Scenarios.</b> "Current trend" extends each economy's 10-year growth differential against the high-income group. "Peer pace" applies the 80th-percentile differential achieved by low- and middle-income economies over the same window. Both are mechanical extrapolations, not forecasts.</p>
