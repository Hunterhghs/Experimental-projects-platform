<h2 id="chapter-01"><span class="chapter-num">Chapter 01</span>Food Systems as the Polycrisis Transmission Engine</h2>

<h3 id="the-polycrisis-nexus-and-synchronous-breadbasket-vulnerability">The Polycrisis Nexus: Synchronous Shocks, Breadbasket Vulnerabilities, and Compounding Insecurity</h3>

<p>Within the converging architecture of the global polycrisis, food systems operate not merely as passive recipients of climatic damage, but as the primary transmission engine of civilizational instability. When extreme heat, drought, floods, soil exhaustion, fertilizer price surges, and crop diseases strike agricultural regions, the resulting failure does not remain confined to rural farming communities. Instead, it instantly amplifies into acute malnutrition, poverty spikes, violent food-price inflation, political unrest, and mass involuntary displacement.</p>

<p>The foundational insight underscored by recent Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) synthesis reports is that simultaneous crop losses across major producing regions are especially consequential because international trade cannot compensate for correlated supply shocks. For decades, international food security models relied on a core assumption of geographic portfolio diversification: the belief that a severe drought or localized harvest collapse in one hemisphere would be balanced by a bumper harvest elsewhere through open global trade.</p>

<p>Under planetary heating, that foundational assumption has collapsed. Planetary warming is destabilizing the northern hemisphere circumglobal jet stream, amplifying quasi-resonant atmospheric Rossby waves (specifically wavenumber 5 and wavenumber 7). These stationary thermodynamic meanders lock persistent heat domes and severe drought anomalies simultaneously across the world's primary breadbaskets—the North American grain belt, Western Europe, Ukraine, and the Russian steppes. When fewer than five sovereign nations control over 73% of global wheat exports, 82% of maize exports, 84% of rice exports, and 89% of soybean exports, synchronous harvest failure destroys international buffer capacity, precipitating catastrophic global price contagion that pushes fragile developing sovereigns into political collapse.</p>

<h3 id="compound-thermal-extremes-staple-yield-declines-and-sovereign-in">Compound Thermal Extremes, Staple Yield Declines, and Sovereign Inflation Transmission</h3>

<p>The foundational stability of the global macroeconomic order is inextricably anchored in the biophysical productivity of the planetary food system. Yet as global mean surface temperatures advance past critical thresholds, compound drought-heat extremes are destabilizing major breadbaskets across the Global South. Over 500 million smallholder farming families—cultivating less than two hectares each and producing upwards of 70% of food in developing economies—operate on the frontlines of this systemic disruption.</p>

Under an unmitigated +3.0°C shock, staple caloric yields collapse by more than 28% to 32%.</p>

<p>Crucially, the yield sensitivity of traditional C3 cereals (wheat, rice) contrasts sharply with resilient C4 and orphan crops. While maize and wheat experience precipitous yield declines due to pollen sterility and accelerated senescence above 35°C, indigenous drought-hardy crops such as pearl millet, sorghum, and grain amaranth maintain positive yield trajectories under moderate warming, demonstrating the urgent necessity of diversifying national crop portfolios away from monoculture vulnerabilities.</p>

<p>In developing economies, localized harvest failures trigger immediate macroeconomic crises. Because food expenditures account for 35% to 50% of the household consumption basket across low-income populations, localized agricultural supply shocks transmit directly into headline consumer price index (CPI) spikes. This imported and domestic food inflation forces central banks to hike interest rates into contracting economies, depleting sovereign foreign exchange reserves and exacerbating external debt vulnerabilities.</p>

<p>Cross-regional macroeconomic analysis reveals that developing regions with the highest food expenditure shares in consumer price baskets suffer the most severe, destabilizing food price inflation volatility.</p>

<p>In stark contrast, advanced OECD economies allocate only 11.5% of consumer spending to food, experiencing muted inflation volatility of just 4.2%. For developing nations, food price surges do not merely reduce discretionary consumption—they trigger acute caloric deprivation, deplete family savings, and spark urban civil unrest, demonstrating that food system resilience is a non-negotiable prerequisite for sovereign national security and fiscal stability.</p>

<p>Escaping this recurrent macroeconomic trap requires shifting agricultural policy from reactive crisis imports and food subsidies toward structural investments in smallholder productivity, decentralized cold chains, and shock-absorbent logistics that insulate domestic markets from global climate volatility.</p>

<h2 id="chapter-02"><span class="chapter-num">Chapter 02</span>Agronomic Adaptation &amp; Soil Health</h2>

<h3 id="thermal-seed-genetics-c4-orphan-crops-and-regenerative-biochar-i">Thermal Seed Genetics, C4 Orphan Crops, and Regenerative Biochar Integration</h3>

<p>The physical foundation of any shock-absorbent food system begins at the root zone: the biological resilience of crop genetics and the hydro-physical water-holding capacity of the soil. Decades of intensive industrial monoculture across the Global South have severely degraded rural soils, depleting soil organic matter (SOM) below 1% and rendering rainfed farming systems extraordinarily fragile to even minor dry spells and heatwaves.</p>

<p>To withstand an era of climatic volatility, plant breeding must aggressively pivot from maximizing single-variable yield potential under optimal conditions toward maximizing yield stability under compound stress. The international research centers of the CGIAR (such as CIMMYT, ICRISAT, and IRRI) in partnership with National Agricultural Research Systems (NARS) have pioneered the deployment of Drought-Tolerant Maize for Africa (DTMA), heat-tolerant submergence-resilient 'scuba' rice, and bio-fortified pearl millet.</p>

<p>These stress-adapted varieties incorporate physiological traits such as deeper rooting architectures that access subsoil moisture, canopy stomatal closure modulation to minimize daytime evapotranspirative loss, and stay-green characteristics that maintain photosynthetic activity during prolonged dry spells. Crucially, C4 orphan crops—including finger millet, teff, fonio, and cowpeas—possess natural evolutionary adaptations to high temperatures and water stress, utilizing 30% to 50% less water per unit of grain produced than C3 wheat.</p>

<p>However, superior genetic seed architecture is rendered ineffective if planted in depleted, compacted soils devoid of biological sponge capacity. Regenerative agriculture integrates minimal soil tillage, permanent organic cover cropping, and the incorporation of pyrolysis-produced biochar to rebuild soil organic carbon (SOC) levels from 0.8% to over 3.5% across smallholder plots.</p>

<p>Every 1% increase in soil organic carbon expands the soil's moisture retention capacity by approximately 140,000 to 180,000 liters per hectare. This subterranean moisture reservoir acts as a vital temporal buffer, allowing crops to survive two to three weeks of mid-season rainfall hiatuses without leaf rolling or yield abortion, effectively converting erratic monsoon rains into reliable soil moisture capital.</p>

<p>Furthermore, pyrolyzed biochar permanently sequesters atmospheric carbon for centuries while neutralizing soil acidity and serving as a porous scaffold that hosts beneficial mycorrhizal fungi and nitrogen-fixing bacteria, reducing synthetic nitrogen fertilizer requirements by 30% to 45% and eliminating costly input import bills for smallholder farmers.</p>

Where irrigation does exist, it relies overwhelmingly on polluting, expensive diesel-powered generator pumps or wasteful unlined flood channels that exhaust shallow aquifers while consuming up to one-third of farm operating revenues.</p>

<h3 id="hydraulic-micro-emitting-physics-aquifer-protection-and-payg-dis">Hydraulic Micro-Emitting Physics, Aquifer Protection, and PAYG Distribution</h3>

<p>The engineering architecture of smallholder solar irrigation overcomes severe physical and financial barriers through innovative system miniaturization. Traditional commercial drip irrigation systems require high operating pressures (1.5 to 3.0 bar) to overcome frictional pipe losses and maintain emitter uniformity, necessitating large, expensive multi-kilowatt pumping systems. In contrast, smallholder micro-drip kits utilize ultra-low-pressure turbulent flow emitters operating at just 0.15 to 0.40 bar, allowing water to be distributed uniformly across half-acre plots using elevated 500-liter header tanks or direct low-wattage DC solar pumps.</p>

<p>A critical engineering requirement is robust filtration. Shallow groundwater and open farm ponds in developing rural basins contain high concentrations of suspended silt, biological algae, and dissolved iron. Modern smallholder kits integrate dual-stage screen and disc filters with self-flushing valves, preventing emitter clogging and maintaining hydraulic distribution uniformity above 90% over multi-year operational lifetimes with minimal maintenance labor.</p>

<p>However, deploying zero-marginal-cost solar pumping at massive scale carries a severe ecological hazard: the risk of accelerating groundwater overdraft. Because solar electricity is free once the capital equipment is installed, farmers face zero marginal cost for running pumps continuously, which can rapidly exhaust fragile shallow water tables. To prevent this tragedy of the commons, modern solar pumps integrate IoT telemetry, GSM data transmitters, and automated soil matric potential shut-off switches that cease pumping once field capacity is achieved.</p>

<p>The decisive catalyst enabling mass smallholder adoption has been the Pay-As-You-Go (PAYG) financing model, pioneered by agritech innovators like SunCulture, Futurepump, and Khethworks. Historically, smallholders were locked out of solar irrigation by the upfront $600 to $1,000 capital cost, which exceeded their entire annual cash savings, while traditional commercial banks refused to lend without formal land title collateral.</p>

<p>Under the PAYG model, the smallholder pays a modest upfront deposit ($50 to $100), and the equipment is immediately installed. The farmer repays the remaining capital cost through flexible daily or monthly mobile money installments ($1.00 to $1.50/day)—amounts substantially lower than their previous daily diesel fuel purchases. Integrated smart IoT controllers allow distributors to disable the pump remotely if installments lapse, creating a self-collateralizing asset structure that compresses default rates below 3%.</p>

<h3 id="biological-respiration-kinetics-farm-gate-value-destruction-and">Biological Respiration Kinetics, Farm-Gate Value Destruction, and Methane Emissions</h3>

<p>While international development dialogues focus obsessively on expanding agricultural production, the most tragic scandal of the global agrifood system occurs after the harvest: between 30% and 50% of all perishable food produced across emerging economies is wasted before it ever reaches a consumer's plate. In a warming century, post-harvest food loss represents an unmitigated humanitarian, economic, and ecological disaster.</p>

<p>The physical mechanics of post-harvest perishability are governed by plant biochemical respiration kinetics and Arrhenius temperature dependencies. Once a fruit, vegetable, or tuber is harvested, it is severed from the parent plant's water and nutrient supply, but remains a living biological organism. The harvested crop consumes its own stored sugars and organic acids through respiration to maintain cellular metabolism, releasing heat, water vapor, and carbon dioxide.</p>

<p>Crucially, the rate of biological respiration doubles or triples with every 10°C increase in ambient temperature (a Q10 temperature coefficient between 2.0 and 3.2). In tropical emerging economies where harvested produce is left exposed in unshaded open fields or roadside markets under blistering ambient temperatures of 32°C to 40°C, biological respiration proceeds at hyper-accelerated velocity, causing rapid moisture desiccation, cellular breakdown, and ethylene-induced senescence within 24 to 48 hours.</p>

<p>This thermal decay is rapidly colonized by opportunistic bacterial and fungal pathogens, inducing soft rot, mold growth, and mycotoxin contamination. In dairy value chains, raw milk leaves the cow at 37°C—the optimal incubation temperature for bacterial proliferation. Without rapid cooling to below 4°C within two hours of milking, microbial counts double every 20 minutes, souring millions of liters of milk before smallholders can transport it to urban processing dairies.</p>

<p>The macroeconomic toll of this post-harvest leakage is staggering. In Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, post-harvest losses destroy more than $95 billion in economic value annually, representing the forfeiture of 25% to 40% of smallholder gross farm revenues. Farmers are forced into 'distress dumping'—selling their bumper crops to predatory middlemen for pennies on the dollar immediately after harvest because they lack the physical infrastructure to store their produce.</p>

<p>Furthermore, food loss carries an immense environmental footprint: wasting 250 cubic kilometers of irrigation water and 1.4 billion hectares of land, while decomposing organic waste in open municipal dumps generates millions of tons of fugitive methane, accelerating the planetary warming that caused the crisis in the first place.</p>

<h3 id="decentralized-solar-cold-rooms-phase-change-materials-and-coolin">Decentralized Solar Cold Rooms, Phase Change Materials, and Cooling-as-a-Service</h3>

<p>Closing the post-harvest perishable food gap cannot rely on the centralized, grid-dependent cold chain architectures of advanced economies. Across rural farming belts in Africa, South Asia, and Latin America, electricity grids are either entirely absent, prohibitively expensive to extend, or subject to chronic blackouts lasting 8 to 16 hours daily. <p>The breakthrough technological solution is the modular, off-grid solar walk-in cold room, pioneered by social enterprises such as ColdHubs in Nigeria, Ecozen in India, and InspiraFarms in Kenya. Installed directly within rural aggregation markets and farm-gate clusters, these standardized 3- to 10-ton cold rooms integrate rooftop solar PV arrays (5 to 15 kWp), high-efficiency DC variable-speed inverter compressors, and ultra-insulated polyurethane foam panels (100mm to 150mm thickness).</p>

<p>A critical thermodynamic innovation is the replacement of toxic, expensive electrochemical lead-acid or lithium battery banks with Phase Change Material (PCM) thermal energy storage. During sunny peak hours, surplus solar power drives the refrigeration unit to freeze a bank of eutectic saltwater or organic paraffin PCM plates lining the interior ceiling and walls. During the night or cloudy spells, the frozen PCM plates gradually melt, passively absorbing heat and maintaining room temperatures strictly between 4°C and 10°C with 85% to 95% relative humidity for up to 36 hours with zero electrical power.</p>

<p>Furthermore, modern cold rooms comply with the Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol by transitioning entirely to natural refrigerants—primarily propane (R290) and carbon dioxide (R744)—which possess near-zero Global Warming Potential (GWP &lt; 3) and zero Ozone Depletion Potential (ODP), completely bypassing high-GWP hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) that contribute to global warming.</p>

<p>To overcome the smallholder capital barrier, operators deploy the Cooling-as-a-Service (CaaS) business model. Smallholder farmers and market women cannot afford to purchase an $18,000 cold room; under CaaS, they pay a nominal daily rental fee—typically $0.10 to $0.20 per 20-kilogram plastic crate per day—to store their unsold produce. Farmers can safely store their vegetables for up to 21 days, waiting for market gluts to subside and selling at peak prices.</p>

<p>Rigorous field evaluations prove that utilizing CaaS solar cold rooms increases smallholder net monthly income by 50% to 75% by eliminating spoilage losses and restoring market bargaining power to rural women traders.</p>

<h2 id="chapter-08"><span class="chapter-num">Chapter 08</span>Warehouse Receipt Systems &amp; Finance</h2>

<h3 id="certified-hermetic-silos-digital-negotiable-receipts-and-distres">Certified Hermetic Silos, Digital Negotiable Receipts, and Distress Sale Mitigation</h3>

<p>In non-perishable grain and pulse value chains—such as maize, rice, wheat, and soybeans—the primary post-harvest crisis is not immediate physical decay, but seasonal financial distress. At the moment of harvest, hundreds of thousands of smallholders harvest simultaneously, flooding local markets with grain. Local commodity prices collapse by 40% to 60%, forcing cash-starved farmers to sell their produce at rock-bottom distress prices to repay immediate debts.</p>

<p>Six months later, during the lean season prior to the next harvest, rural grain supplies dry up and market prices soar. Smallholders, having liquidated their entire harvest at distress prices, are forced to buy back grain for their own families' consumption at two to three times the price they sold it for. This seasonal price whipsaw—the 'sell low, buy high' cycle—is a principal driver of chronic rural indebtedness and intergenerational poverty across emerging economies.</p>

<p>The institutional and financial mechanism to break this cycle is the certified Warehouse Receipt System (WRS). Under a statutory WRS framework, smallholders deposit their harvested grain at a licensed, professionally managed commercial warehouse. The grain is cleaned, graded, moisture-tested, and treated against insect pests, then stored in hermetic Purdue Improved Crop Storage (PICS) bags or climate-controlled steel silos that prevent weevil damage and aflatoxin mold contamination.</p>

<p>Upon deposit, the warehouse operator issues a legally binding, negotiable Warehouse Receipt—now increasingly digitized on secure blockchain or national digital public infrastructure registries. The electronic receipt certifies the exact quantity, quality grade, and ownership of the stored commodity. Commercial banks and microfinance institutions recognize this electronic receipt as pristine, liquid collateral, instantly advancing short-term working capital loans equal to 65% to 75% of the grain's current market value.</p>

<p>With cash in hand to pay school fees, medical bills, and prepare their fields for the next planting season, smallholders are no longer forced into distress selling. They retain legal ownership of their grain while it appreciates in the warehouse. Months later, during the lean season when wholesale prices peak, the farmer sells the grain through commodity exchanges (such as the Ethiopia Commodity Exchange or India's e-NAM), repays the warehouse loan, and pockets a substantial net cash profit.</p>

<p>By transforming agricultural inventory into bankable collateral, Warehouse Receipt Systems inject liquid capital into rural economies, dampen seasonal food price volatility, and incentivize private investment in modern storage infrastructure.</p>

<h3 id="multi-strata-agroforestry-microclimate-buffering-and-leguminous">Multi-Strata Agroforestry, Microclimate Buffering, and Leguminous Soil Enrichment</h3>

<p>Industrial monoculture—planting vast expanses of genetically uniform single crops—is inherently vulnerable to climate extremes. When extreme heatwaves strike an open monoculture field, soil surface temperatures can spike past 45°C, sterilizing pollen, baking beneficial soil microbes, and accelerating moisture evaporation. To absorb climatic volatility, food systems must embrace ecological complexity: the deliberate integration of trees, multi-strata canopies, and diverse crop assemblages.</p>

<p>Agroforestry—the intentional integration of woody perennials with agricultural crops and livestock—re-establishes natural thermodynamic and ecological balances. <p>Faidherbia albida possesses an extraordinary phenological adaptation known as 'reverse phenology': it goes dormant and sheds its nitrogen-rich leaves during the rainy season, allowing full sunlight to reach underlying maize, millet, or sorghum crops while depositing tons of organic nitrogen and mulch. During the blistering dry season, the tree grows a dense green canopy that provides vital shade, lowers wind velocities, and pumps deep groundwater to surface soil layers through hydraulic lift.</p>

<p>The microclimate buffering provided by multi-strata agroforestry canopies is profound. Concurrently, reduced wind speeds diminish crop evapotranspirative moisture loss by 25% to 35%, conserving scarce soil water.</p>

<p>Beyond thermal buffering, diversified agroecological systems build biological pest resilience. Homogeneous monocultures are sitting ducks for climate-driven pest surges, such as the fall armyworm (Spodoptera frugiperda) and desert locusts (Schistocerca gregaria). Multi-strata intercropping—such as the 'push-pull' technology pioneered by icipe in Kenya—integrates repellent silverleaf desmodium between maize rows (pushing moths away) and attractive Napier grass on field perimeters (pulling pests to natural traps), eliminating stemborer damage and parasitic Striga weeds with zero chemical pesticides.</p>

<p>By diversifying farm output into timber, fruits, nuts, livestock fodder, and carbon credits, agroforestry systems insulate smallholders from single-crop market failures, establishing an enduring biological shield against planetary warming.</p>

<h3 id="first-loss-credit-guarantees-off-take-securitization-and-npl-com">First-Loss Credit Guarantees, Off-Take Securitization, and NPL Compression</h3>

<p>The fundamental structural barrier preventing commercial banks from lending to smallholder agriculture is perceived credit risk. Commercial lenders perceive smallholder farming as an uninsurable, high-risk sector characterized by weather dependency, informal land tenure, and lack of audited financial records. Consequently, commercial bank lending to agriculture hovers below 3% to 5% of total bank portfolios across emerging markets, while interest rates for rural microcredit frequently exceed 30% to 45% annually.</p>

<p>To break this structural credit paralysis, Multilateral Development Banks (MDBs) and sovereign development finance institutions (DFIs) must deploy First-Loss Credit Guarantee Facilities. Under this structure, the MDB provides a funded 10% to 15% first-loss guarantee pool to participating commercial banks. If a smallholder default occurs due to localized climate shocks, the first-loss pool absorbs the initial losses, shielding the commercial bank's balance sheet.</p>

<p>Crucially, empirical structured finance modeling demonstrates that providing a 10% first-loss credit guarantee unlocks an 8x to 10x commercial lending volume multiplier. Commercial banks, insulated from catastrophic default, expand agricultural lending portfolios, extend loan tenors from 6 months to 36 months, and slash smallholder interest rates from 36% down to competitive commercial rates of 12% to 14%, enabling farmers to finance high-return assets like solar drip irrigation and hermetic silos.</p>

<p>Furthermore, credit risk is structurally compressed through 'Off-Take Securitization' in partnership with digital B2B agritech platforms. Rather than disbursing unmonitored cash loans, the lender disburses digital vouchers directly to verified input suppliers for certified stress-tolerant seeds and precision irrigation equipment. The smallholder signs an off-take purchase agreement with the digital platform.</p>

<p>When the crop is harvested and collected at the platform's packhouse, the loan repayment is automatically deducted at the source from the digital off-take settlement escrow, and the remaining net profit is transferred instantly to the farmer's mobile money wallet. This closed-loop financial architecture eliminates cash diversion, driving portfolio Non-Performing Loan (NPL) rates below 2%.</p>

<aside class="takeaway"><span class="takeaway__label">Executive takeaway: de-risking smallholder credit rails</span>
<p><strong>10% First-Loss Pool Unlocks 10x Commercial Capital MDB guarantees insulate commercial banks, expanding agricultural loan books.: </strong>Interest rates drop from predatory 36%+ microfinance rates to 12%–14% commercial bank rates for productive asset investments.</p>
<p><strong>Closed-Loop Off-Take Securitization Digital platform off-take agreements deduct loan service automatically at the source.: </strong>Eliminating cash leakage and repayment friction compresses smallholder portfolio NPL default rates below 2.0%.</p>
</aside>

<h2 id="chapter-13"><span class="chapter-num">Chapter 13</span>Institutional Governance &amp; Land Tenure</h2>

<h3 id="customary-property-formalization-digital-cadastres-and-regional">Customary Property Formalization, Digital Cadastres, and Regional Trade Corridors</h3>

<p>The most sophisticated climate-resilient technologies—drip irrigation networks, multi-strata agroforestry, and hermetic grain silos—will never be adopted at scale if the institutional and legal foundation of land tenure remains insecure. A farmer who does not have legal certainty that they will control their plot five years from now will never invest their scarce capital in planting perennial shade trees, building soil organic matter, or installing subsurface irrigation pipes.</p>

<p>Across the developing world, more than 70% of rural land is held under informal, customary, or unregistered tenure arrangements. Smallholder farmers, particularly women, lack formal land titles or recognized deeds. This legal informality leaves farming communities vulnerable to predatory land grabs, expropriation, and eviction, directly disincentivizing long-term capital investments in on-farm climate resilience.</p>

<p>Resolving this tenure insecurity requires low-cost, decentralized land administration utilizing digital cadastre mapping and participatory boundary demarcation. Using handheld GNSS receivers, drone spatial surveys, and mobile GIS applications, community land registries can map and formalize customary land rights at a cost of less than $10 per parcel—a 90% cost reduction compared to legacy bureaucratic surveying—granting smallholders statutory Certificates of Customary Ownership.</p>

<p>Legal formalization must explicitly mandate equal property and inheritance rights for women. In Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, women provide more than 50% of agricultural labor, yet own less than 15% of agricultural land. Ensuring joint spousal titling unlocks immense productivity gains: empirical studies prove that when women farmers gain secure land tenure, on-farm soil conservation investments double and household nutritional outcomes surge.</p>

<p>Beyond domestic land governance, regional agrifood resilience requires eliminating intra-regional trade barriers. Food crises in emerging economies are frequently exacerbated by arbitrary export bans, corrupt border checkpoints, and opaque Sanitary and Phytosanitary (SPS) regulations that strand food trucks at borders for days, causing tons of perishables to rot in customs yards.</p>

<p>Accelerating the implementation of regional free trade frameworks—most notably the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA)—and establishing digitized 'Green Freight Lanes' with standardized electronic SPS certificates enables surplus food from bumper-harvest regions to flow seamlessly across borders to deficit regions, stabilizing regional food prices and neutralizing localized climate shocks.</p>

<aside class="takeaway"><span class="takeaway__label">Executive takeaway: institutional tenure &amp; trade pillars</span>
<p><strong>Secure Land Tenure Unlocks On-Farm Investment Formal customary land certificates double smallholder soil and irrigation investments.: </strong>Decentralized digital cadastre mapping with drone GIS registers rural land parcels for less than $10 per farm.</p>
<p><strong>Mandatory Gender Titling Equity Granting women joint legal land ownership surges household nutritional investments by 40%.: </strong>AfCFTA Green Freight Lanes eliminate border delays, allowing regional food surpluses to absorb localized drought shocks.</p>
