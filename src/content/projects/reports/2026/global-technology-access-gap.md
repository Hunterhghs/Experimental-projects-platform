The polycrisis is not waiting for better technology. Early-warning systems, off-grid solar, drought-tolerant seed, efficient cooling, digital payments and low-cost water treatment all exist, and most of them are falling in price. What is missing is deployment: the gap between the technologies that work and the people who can actually use them. That gap, not the pace of invention, will decide who absorbs the climate-pollution shocks of this century and who is broken by them.

<h2 id="chapter-01">Chapter 1: The Access Gap Is the Binding Constraint on Resilience</h2>

Resilience is often framed as a research problem — a search for the next breakthrough that will protect populations from heat, floods and polluted air. The framing is wrong. The binding constraint is access.

Consider the basic arithmetic. The International Energy Agency and World Bank count 675 million people still living without electricity in 2023. The International Telecommunication Union counts 2.6 billion people offline. The World Meteorological Organization reports that only half of the world's countries have full multi-hazard early warning coverage. Each of these populations faces the same polycrisis hazards as everyone else. They face them without the tools that convert a hazard into a manageable event.

<div class="takeaway">
<span class="takeaway__label">The Deployment Principle</span>
<p><strong>Every proven resilience technology that reaches less than universal coverage is an adaptation subsidy sitting unclaimed.</strong> The invention already happened; the delivery has not.</p>
</div>

The distinction matters because the two problems require different instruments. Invention responds to R&D spending, venture capital and patent systems. Deployment responds to infrastructure finance, last-mile distribution, affordability and maintenance. Treating a deployment gap as if it were an invention gap misdirects capital toward laboratories when the highest-return investment is a transformer, a cell tower, or a trained local technician.

<h2 id="chapter-02">Chapter 2: Mapping the Resilience Technology Stack</h2>

The resilience technology stack has five load-bearing layers. Each layer performs a distinct shock-absorption function, and each layer shows a measurable access deficit.

Electricity sits at the base of the stack. It powers water pumping, vaccine refrigeration, cooling, connectivity and hospital equipment. At 91% global coverage, it is also the most widely deployed layer — yet the missing 9% concentrates in exactly the regions where heat and flood exposure are rising fastest.

Clean cooking follows. Roughly 2.3 billion people still burn wood, charcoal, dung or kerosene indoors. Household air pollution from these fuels kills an estimated 3.2 million people a year, most of them women and children. Clean cooking is the cheapest air-quality intervention available, and its absence keeps a large fraction of humanity inside a self-inflicted pollution crisis even before ambient pollution is counted.

Connectivity forms the third layer. Internet access delivers early warnings, telemedicine, remote work and mobile money. The 2.6 billion people offline are excluded from all of these simultaneously. Digital finance deserves separate notice: 76% of adults now hold a bank or mobile-money account, which means a quarter of adults still transact in cash during precisely the moments that matter most: evacuation, remittance and emergency borrowing.

Water and early warning complete the stack. Some 73% of the world's population uses safely managed drinking water, leaving 2.2 billion people exposed to contaminated sources that turn floods into cholera outbreaks. And the WMO's finding that only half of countries operate full multi-hazard early warning systems means that the cheapest resilience technology on earth, a forecast delivered before the hazard arrives, still misses half the planet's jurisdictions.

The geography of the deficit is not random. South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa together hold roughly two-thirds of the unelectrified population, the largest clean-cooking deficit, and the fastest-growing compound heat-and-flood exposure. The access gap and the hazard map overlap almost perfectly — which is why the gap is a resilience problem, not a residual development statistic.

<h2 id="chapter-03">Chapter 3: How the Polycrisis Compounds the Access Gap</h2>

Left alone, an access gap does not stay constant. Compound shocks widen it, through three mechanisms that stack on each other.

The first mechanism is asset destruction without replacement. A household without electricity and without mobile money holds its wealth in cash, livestock and unreinforced structures. A flood destroys all three. Recovery then requires buying back the same assets at post-disaster prices, usually through informal lenders at predatory rates. A connected household in the same flood zone receives an early warning, moves assets, receives a digital transfer, and rebuilds faster. The same hazard produces divergence, not convergence.

The second mechanism is coping debt. Families facing a shock without insurance or digital credit sell productive assets (a pump, a sewing machine, a generator) to buy food. Each sale reduces the household's future income, which makes the next shock harder to survive. This is the compound vulnerability multiplier in operation: exposure and lost productive capacity multiply each other, and the multiplication runs fastest where technology access is thinnest.

The third mechanism is institutional. Governments without early-warning infrastructure, digital registries and electrified clinics cannot target assistance. Aid arrives late, in cash, to the wrong places. Every failed response deepens the population's distrust in formal systems, pushing more people toward informal arrangements that reinforce the gap. The access deficit thus functions as a self-reinforcing loop: weak technology access weakens institutions, and weak institutions delay the deployment of technology.

<h2 id="chapter-04">Chapter 4: The Counter-Case: Access Follows Income</h2>

The strongest opposing read holds that the access gap closes automatically as countries grow. In this view, electricity, connectivity and clean cooking are consumption goods that households buy when incomes rise, so the correct policy is growth first and access second.

The evidence argues otherwise. Growth itself is shock-vulnerable in the absence of access. An economy whose labor force burns biomass indoors loses worker health; an economy whose firms go offline in a flood loses output; an economy whose population cannot receive digital transfers recovers slower after every disaster. The World Bank estimates that each dollar invested in resilient infrastructure avoids four dollars in disaster losses. Access is therefore a precondition for the growth that is supposed to deliver access — a circularity that resolves in favor of investment, not waiting.

The historical record reinforces the point. India reached near-universal electricity connections through a state-led campaign, not through rising incomes alone. Kenya built the world's leading mobile-money market while still a lower-middle-income country. Both cases show that access can run ahead of income when it is treated as infrastructure. Where it is treated as a private purchase, deficits persist for decades even in middle-income economies.

<h2 id="chapter-05">Chapter 5: Closing the Gap Is the Cheapest Resilience Investment</h2>

The most consequential fact about the access gap is its price. Closing the core stack costs less than the losses its absence guarantees.

Universal early warning costs roughly $600 million a year — a figure smaller than the annual budget of a mid-sized city. Universal clean cooking costs about $8 billion a year. Universal electricity requires about $30 billion a year, and universal broadband roughly $50 billion a year. Together the core stack costs on the order of $90 billion annually.

Set that number against the alternatives. Global disaster losses run around $300 billion a year, and the UNEP adaptation finance gap sits between $194 billion and $366 billion a year. The access gap is not one more item on the adaptation bill; it is the cheapest line on it, and the one that makes every other line cheaper. Early warning systems deliver an estimated ten-to-one return. Resilient infrastructure returns four-to-one. No comparable return exists elsewhere in the adaptation portfolio.

This is why technology access should be read as pre-adaptation. It is the fixed, knowable investment that lowers the marginal cost of every resilience measure that follows — better housing, better insurance, better evacuation, better recovery. The polycrisis will not be resolved by a laboratory breakthrough that arrives in 2040. It will be resolved, or not, by the speed at which existing tools reach the 675 million people still in the dark, the 2.6 billion still offline, and the half of the world's countries still without early warning.

**Binding term:** Treat technology access as the denominator of polycrisis resilience, not a development input. Judge every resilience investment by how many exposed people it reaches — not by how advanced the intervention is.
