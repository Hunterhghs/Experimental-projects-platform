Technology transfer is described as a shipment: blueprints, patents and machinery move from a firm that has them to a firm that does not, and the receiving economy becomes more productive. The description is wrong. Knowledge is embedded in routines, in the tacit judgment of engineers, in supplier discipline, in a bank's willingness to lend against an unfamiliar process. What crosses a border is a partial encoding of that knowledge; the receiving firm must reconstruct the rest from its own capabilities. The transaction is the beginning of the transfer, not the transfer itself.

<h2 id="chapter-01">Chapter 1: Transfer Is Not a Transaction</h2>

<p>The scale of the flows makes the distinction consequential. Global foreign direct investment inflows were about $1.3 trillion in 2023, according to UNCTAD, dwarfing official development assistance and every dedicated technology-transfer facility combined. Yet the capital is not evenly spread, and technology-intensive FDI is more concentrated still: a handful of economies absorb the overwhelming majority of investment in semiconductors, pharmaceuticals and digital infrastructure. The rest of the world receives investment that is largely market-seeking or resource-seeking, with modest technological content. A country can record respectable inflows for a decade and find its industrial capabilities barely moved.</p>

<p>The same concentration appears in the payments that measure disembodied technology trade. Royalty and licence fee receipts are dominated by the United States, Germany and Japan; China has climbed into the second tier, and the rest of the world shares a residual small relative to its population and output. These receipts are the visible price of knowledge, and their distribution maps who owns the codified frontier. It does not map who is learning. Learning shows up elsewhere: in the export basket, in the domestic value added of assembly, in the number of engineers who can debug a process rather than merely operate it.</p>

<p>This is why the policy conversation so often disappoints. Governments negotiate for access, build parks to attract investors and count the capital that arrives, while the binding constraint sits inside domestic firms. The World Bank's 2024 World Development Report makes the point structurally: development is a ladder from investment to infusion to innovation. Investment builds the base. Infusion is the harder rung, where firms adopt and adapt technologies already in use elsewhere. Innovation is where a country pushes the frontier itself. Most countries can climb the first rung with capital and stability; many stall on the second, because infusion is a capability problem rather than a financing problem, and capability cannot be purchased in a single transaction.</p>

<div class="takeaway">
<span class="takeaway__label">The Transfer Principle</span>
<p><strong>Technology transfer succeeds when it changes what domestic firms can do, not what they own.</strong> A solar farm installed with imported panels and foreign technicians transfers an asset; it does not transfer a capability.</p>
</div>

<h2 id="chapter-02">Chapter 2: The Channels: FDI, Licensing, Imitation, People</h2>

<p>Four channels carry technology across borders, and they behave differently. Foreign direct investment brings process technology, quality systems and management practice with the capital. The direct effect on the affiliate is large and immediate; the spillover to the surrounding economy is what development policy cares about, and it is far less reliable. Spillovers arrive through demonstration, labour mobility and supply-chain linkage, and each route requires a domestic counterpart capable of receiving what is offered.</p>

<p>Licensing is the cheapest formal route. A firm pays for the right to use a patent or process and receives documentation, often with training. Its weakness is that licensors transfer the minimum necessary to collect the fee and retain the tacit knowledge that makes the process work at the frontier. Licensing built South Korea in the 1960s and 1970s, but only because the licensee invested heavily in understanding what it had bought.</p>

<p>Imitation operates without permission: reverse engineering, patent reading, hiring diaspora engineers, studying imported goods. It is legally contested and works better in mechanical and electrical products than in advanced materials or biologics, where tacit process knowledge is decisive. It is nonetheless a real channel, and countries that pretend otherwise write policies that ignore how their own firms learn.</p>

<p>People are the channel most often underweighted. Technology moves in heads: students who return, engineers who spend five years inside a multinational and then join a domestic firm, managers who learn a quality system and install it elsewhere. Korea, Taiwan and China all relied on this channel heavily. It is slow, it depends on domestic employers worth returning to, and it is easily disrupted by visa regimes and suspicion of the diaspora.</p>

<p>The channels interact, and the interaction produces upgrading. Vietnam's industrial parks illustrate the combination: foreign investment in electronics created trained workers and a supplier network; domestic firms began in low-value components and moved upward as their capabilities and their customers' demands rose together. None of this happened because a park was built. It happened because the park was accompanied by firms and workers who could absorb what the investors brought.</p>

<h2 id="chapter-03">Chapter 3: Why Spillovers Fail: The Absorptive-Capacity Constraint</h2>

<p>The most robust finding in the empirical literature is that spillovers are conditional. They occur when domestic firms have the capacity to absorb, adapt and improve what foreign firms bring. That capacity has three components: firm-level R&D, which supplies the technical staff and experimental habit needed to make sense of an imported process; a supply of skilled engineers; and management quality, the ability to plan, measure, maintain and organise production reliably. Absorptive capacity is the difference between a firm that can run a licensed line and a firm that can improve it.</p>

<p>The evidence is extensive. Studies of FDI spillovers find positive effects concentrated among domestic firms that already invest in R&D and employ skilled workers, and negligible or negative effects elsewhere. Where a foreign affiliate enters and domestic firms cannot absorb, it competes for the best local workers and can leave the local economy worse positioned. FDI complements domestic capability; it does not substitute for it.</p>

<p>The aggregate numbers show how unevenly the inputs are distributed. South Korea invests 4.9 percent of GDP in R&D, the highest rate in the world; China invests about 2.4 percent, having roughly tripled its intensity over two decades; the OECD average is about 2.7 percent. Most middle-income countries spend under 1 percent, and many low-income countries a fraction of that. These gaps compound: a country spending 0.3 percent of GDP on R&D is not merely investing less than Korea; it is failing to build the technical workforce, the laboratory infrastructure and the firm-level habits that make imported technology usable.</p>

<div class="callout">
<span class="callout-title">Absorptive capacity depreciates</span>
<p>A firm that licensed a technology in 1995 and never invested in understanding it does not hold a capability in 2025; it holds obsolete drawings and a workforce that has forgotten why the process worked. <strong>Upgrading is a continuous expenditure, not a phase to be completed.</strong></p>
</div>

<p>The policy consequence is that supply-side programmes (attracting investors, negotiating licences, building parks) address the easier half of the problem. The harder half is creating firms that can use what is on offer: funding firm-level R&D even when it looks inefficient in the short run, building engineering education at scale, and investing in management quality, which is cheap relative to its returns and almost universally neglected. The countries that climbed the ladder did all three, simultaneously and for decades.</p>

<h2 id="chapter-04">Chapter 4: The Escaper Playbook: South Korea, China, Vietnam</h2>

<p>The countries that moved from technology recipient to technology creator share a sequence. First, import technology deliberately, using market access, procurement or joint-venture rules to obtain not just equipment but training and documentation. Second, invest heavily in the capacity to understand what has been imported. Third, convert that understanding into domestic production, first for the home market and then for export. Fourth, begin generating technology at the frontier, where the earlier investments finally pay.</p>

<p>South Korea is the clearest case. In the 1960s it licensed processes it could not develop and assembled for foreign buyers; over four decades it moved from licensing foreign technology to exporting it, and it now spends 4.9 percent of GDP on R&D. The transition was not market-driven in any simple sense. The state directed credit to large firms, protected the domestic market long enough for them to learn, and insisted on export performance as the test of whether the learning was real.</p>

<p>China followed a different route to a similar destination. Its joint-venture arrangements forced technology into the domestic economy, and they worked imperfectly but substantially: domestic partners learned process discipline, trained engineers, and built the capability to substitute domestic components for imported ones. China now invests about 2.4 percent of GDP in R&D and files the largest annual volume of patents in the world. The patent count is not a measure of frontier innovation, but it measures a large population of firms and engineers engaged in technical work — the absorptive capacity the earlier strategy was built to create.</p>

<p>Vietnam is an earlier stage of the same sequence, and instructive because it is incomplete. Its industrial parks absorbed foreign investment in electronics and higher-value assembly, and the country moved up the value chain as investors demanded more sophisticated local content. But Vietnam's R&D intensity remains around 0.5 percent of GDP, which is the central risk in its trajectory: it has built the investment rung convincingly, while the infusion rung requires firm-level capability that low R&D spending does not produce. Whether Vietnam escapes depends on whether it converts foreign-invested production into domestic technical capability before the cost advantage that attracted the investment erodes.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">
The playbook is not a list of instruments to be copied; it is a discipline (import, understand, produce, generate) sustained across decades and through political cycles. Countries that treat transfer as a transaction stop at the first step.
</div>

<h2 id="chapter-05">Chapter 5: The Counter-Case: Open Up and Technology Will Flow</h2>

<p>The strongest opposing view holds that none of this policy apparatus is necessary. Technology flows to wherever it can be used productively, so the binding constraint is openness, not absorptive capacity: remove tariffs, allow foreign ownership, protect property rights, and technology will arrive because it is profitable for its owners to bring it. The view has real support. The fastest-growing economies of the late twentieth century all opened to trade and investment, foreign firms do bring technology when they build plants, and licensing flows respond to the strength of intellectual property protection.</p>

<p>The refutation is empirical. If openness alone sufficed, the correlation between FDI inflows and domestic capability growth would be strong, and it is not. Many economies have been open for decades (low tariffs, unrestricted foreign ownership, functioning courts) and have received substantial investment without generating domestic technological capability. Openness is necessary for transfer; it is not sufficient for absorption. The R&D numbers make the point: Korea's 4.9 percent and China's 2.4 percent are not the product of openness but of deliberate, sustained investment in the capacity to understand technology. Most middle-income countries liberalised and did not catch up, because the constraint was internal.</p>

<div class="callout callout--warn">
<span class="callout-title">Openness and capability are complements</span>
<p>A closed economy starves its firms of technology; an open economy without absorptive capacity starves them of the ability to use it. <strong>The sequencing is the policy:</strong> Korea and China protected learning firms until they could compete, then exposed them to export discipline.</p>
</div>

<p>The openness view also misreads the East Asian record. Korea and China opened selectively, and where countries opened abruptly without building absorptive capacity first, the result was often the displacement of domestic firms rather than their upgrading, because foreign entrants outcompeted local producers before those producers had learned enough to compete.</p>

<h2 id="chapter-06">Chapter 6: Green Industrial Upgrading in the Clean Transition</h2>

<p>The clean transition has made technology transfer a live geopolitical question. Decarbonisation requires the diffusion of solar manufacturing, batteries, electrolysers and grid equipment at a speed and scale without precedent, and most of that diffusion must happen in countries that do not currently manufacture the equipment. The UNFCCC process has long recognised technology transfer as an obligation of the developed world; the clean transition gives that obligation operational weight. The question is no longer whether technology should move but who pays for it, who owns the resulting capacity, and whether recipient countries end up as manufacturers or merely as customers.</p>

<p>The instruments now in use are contested. The US Inflation Reduction Act and the EU Green Deal attach local-content requirements to domestic clean-manufacturing subsidies. These are defended as necessary to build supply chains and sustain political coalitions for decarbonisation, and criticised as protectionist and discriminatory against developing-country producers. The contest has a specific consequence: local-content requirements can force production into the subsidising country, reducing the diffusion of manufacturing capability to the places that need it most.</p>

<p>For developing countries, the strategic question is the one Korea and Vietnam faced, in a new sector and at a faster tempo. Clean technology manufacturing is capital-intensive and moves quickly down cost curves, so the window for entering at low value and climbing is short. Countries that attract clean-technology investment without building absorptive capacity will host assembly and lose the chance to move up; countries that pair investment attraction with firm-level R&D, engineering education and management upgrading can establish positions in components, materials and services that persist after the initial investment cycle. The investment-infusion-innovation ladder applies directly: the clean transition offers an unusually large infusion opportunity, because the incumbents' advantage is not yet entrenched, but it rewards only those with the capability to absorb.</p>

<p>This report synthesises cross-country evidence on FDI spillovers, technology licensing and industrial upgrading. R&D intensity figures come from UNESCO Institute for Statistics and OECD data; FDI figures from UNCTAD's World Investment Report 2024; royalty and licence fee receipts from World Bank World Development Indicators. </div>

**Binding term:** Treat absorptive capacity (firm-level R&D, skilled engineers and management quality) as the primary object of technology-transfer policy, and fund it directly rather than assuming it follows from investment inflows or market opening.
