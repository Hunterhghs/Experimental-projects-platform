Agricultural Technology and Global Food Security

The world already grows enough calories to feed everyone. The problem is that the tools to grow them are not on the farms that need them.

The next decade of food security will be decided less by invention than by delivery — moving proven agronomy to the fields, regions and seasons where the yield gap still lives.

Abstract

Feeding 9.7 billion people by 2050 will require roughly 50 per cent more food, feed and biofuel than the world produced in 2012 — and far more in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, where hunger is concentrated and yields sit far below their potential. The binding constraint is no longer total production. It is delivery: moving proven agricultural technology to the fields that lack it.

The technologies are not exotic. Drought-tolerant seed, fertilizer timed to the soil, drip irrigation, digital agronomy, better storage and cold chains are mature and widely proven. What separates a two-tonne maize field in sub-Saharan Africa from an eleven-tonne field in the United States is an input and knowledge frontier: seed, nutrients, water control, information and the finance to pay for them.

The economics favour the smallholder over the headline investor. Five hundred million farms under two hectares (about 85 per cent of all farms) work a sliver of the world's land but supply most of the food where hunger is deepest. Every adoption decision on those farms is a bet against rain, price and time. Technology reaches them only when the bet is redesigned: cheaper up front, guaranteed against failure, delivered through people they already trust.

The report closes on a single rule. Measure every agricultural intervention the way the field does: does it move a proven practice from the shelf to the soil?

Executive Summary

Hunger has stopped falling. The tools to reverse it already exist, and they are concentrated on the wrong farms.

Finding 01

The food system must grow more in the places that currently grow least

Demand rises most where yields are lowest. Feeding 2050 is not a global arithmetic problem; it is a delivery problem concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.

Finding 02

The yield gap is the cheapest food-security asset on earth

Sub-Saharan maize averages about two tonnes per hectare against eleven in the United States. The gap reflects missing inputs and knowledge, not missing potential.

Finding 03

Agtech is mature. Its distribution is not

Seed genetics, digital agronomy, precision inputs and cold chains work. What lags is the last mile: extension, finance, storage and the risk-bearing that lets a smallholder try something new.

Finding 04

Climate has become a production variable, and technology is the hedge

Climate change has already shaved roughly a fifth off agricultural productivity growth. Drought- and heat-tolerant varieties, water control and early warning are no longer upgrades; they are baseline requirements.

The headline numbers have been stuck for three years. In 2023 an estimated 733 million people faced hunger, and 2.33 billion (nearly three in ten) were moderately or severely food insecure. The world is off track for 2030, and the drift tracks the yield gap: Africa's hunger prevalence runs above 20 per cent, against a global average near 9 per cent.

Plot 01 · The Arithmetic

Ten Billion Plates

The demand curve and the hunger map are the same map.

The arithmetic of global food security is usually stated as a production challenge: the United Nations projects 9.7 billion people by 2050, and the FAO estimates agriculture must produce almost 50 per cent more food, feed and biofuel than in 2012. Read quickly, the numbers suggest a planet short of calories. Read carefully, they say something more precise. The shortfall is regional, and the regions in question have the weakest farms.

Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia must more than double agricultural output under the FAO's projections; the rest of the world needs roughly a third more. Those two regions also carry the deepest hunger: Africa's undernourishment prevalence exceeds 20 per cent, more than double the global rate. The demand curve and the hunger map are the same map.

Diet change sharpens the arithmetic. As incomes rise, people eat more meat, dairy, fruit and vegetables, and each of those calories costs more land, water and feed than the grain it replaces — pressure the FAO's 50 per cent figure already folds in.

Plot 02 · The Yield Gap

Where the Calories Are Missing

The same crop, on similar soil, produces a fifth of the harvest depending on where it is planted.

The single most important number in global food security is a ratio. Maize in the United States averages about 11.1 tonnes per hectare. The global average is about 5.9 tonnes. Sub-Saharan Africa averages roughly 2.1 tonnes — less than a fifth of the American yield, on land whose agronomic potential is far higher than its current output. Wheat, rice and cassava tell the same story with different digits.

Figure 3 — The maize yield gap Sub-Saharan AfricaWorldUnited States

Better seed, nitrogen, water control and crop management can double yields in many African locations. Sources: PARI No. 8; USDA ERS.

The yield gap has a precise address. Most of Africa's 33 million smallholder maize farms are rain-fed, dependent on rainfall that is increasingly late, weak or violent. Nitrogen application in sub-Saharan Africa remains a fraction of Asian or Western levels, so soil is mined rather than fed. Improved seed reaches a minority of plots. African farmers capture about 20 per cent of their attainable yield; farmers in Europe, Asia and North America capture 56 to 84 per cent.

That gap is the cheapest food-security asset on earth. Growing the missing calories on existing farmland avoids the two expensive alternatives: clearing new land, and importing food. Africa already spends roughly $50 billion a year importing food it could grow closer to home. Every tonne of gap closed is a tonne of imports avoided and a household moved from deficit to surplus.

Read the gap as inventory

The yield gap is an inventory problem: seed, fertilizer, water control, information and finance are under-stocked in the same places. Closing a third of it would change the global hunger map more than any single new invention.

The structure of farming explains the missing inventory: five hundred million farms under two hectares (about 85 per cent of all farms, on roughly 9 per cent of agricultural land) with no collateral, no irrigation buffer, no storage, no extension agent within reach. The next section inventories the technology that exists to serve them.

Plot 03 · The Toolkit

What Agricultural Technology Actually Is

A stack of proven interventions that the world's best farms already use every season.

Agricultural technology suffers from a framing problem. In rich-country headlines it means drones, robots and venture apps. On the farms where food security is decided, it means a seed that survives a dry fortnight, fertilizer placed where the root can reach it, a pump that delivers water without waste, a warehouse that keeps the harvest from rotting. None of this is experimental. All of it is unevenly distributed.

Table 1 — The agtech stack, from seed to sale

Layer What it does Proven where Missing where
Genetics & seed Drought- and heat-tolerant varieties, short-season cultivars, disease resistance. Global maize, wheat and rice systems. Most smallholder plots; improved seed reaches a minority of African fields.
Soil & nutrients Targeted fertilizer, lime to correct acidity, legumes to fix nitrogen. High-yield systems on every continent. Sub-Saharan Africa, where nitrogen use remains a fraction of Asian levels.
Water control Drip and supplementary irrigation, soil-moisture sensing, rainwater capture. Arid and semi-arid commercial farms. Rain-fed smallholder systems that depend on one season's rainfall.
Digital agronomy Soil maps, weather forecasts, satellite monitoring, advisory messages. Large farms with connectivity and capital. The last mile: smallholders reached by phone but rarely by actionable advice.
Post-harvest & cold chain Hermetic storage, drying, refrigerated transport, market linkage. Export supply chains. Domestic chains, where the 14 per cent food-loss figure is concentrated.

The stack is mature at the top of every column. The food-security payoff lies in moving each layer to the "missing where" column.

Post-harvest loss is the most overlooked layer because the calories it destroys were already grown. The FAO estimates about 14 per cent of the world's food is lost between harvest and retail, with Central and Southern Asia losing more than 20 per cent. Hermetic bags and metal silos cut losses sharply; cold chains rescue perishables.

Two conclusions follow. The highest-leverage technologies are unglamorous (seed, soil, water, storage) and already paid for by a century of research. And every layer fails at the same point: the last mile, where a technology must be financed, explained, trusted and maintained on a small farm with no margin for error.

Plot 04 · Precision

Precision as a Public Good

The most valuable data in agriculture describes the fields that can least afford to act on it.

Precision agriculture began as a rich-farm technology: GPS guidance, variable-rate fertilizer, yield monitors. Stripped of the machinery, the same logic serves smallholders. A soil map tells a farmer which corner needs lime. A forecast timed to planting is worth more than a subsidy. A satellite image that shows a crop yellowing two weeks early converts a failed season into an early intervention. The data exists. The delivery does not.

Digital advisory services have grown fast across Africa and South Asia — SMS and voice messages turning agronomic research into plain instructions for a specific crop, soil and week. The best are two-way: a farmer reports a problem, an agronomist or a model answers, on a phone the farmer already owns. The bottleneck is rarely the phone. It is the data and the messenger.

Why this is a public good

One farmer's accurate forecast protects the neighbours who planted the same week. One field's soil data improves the model for every field around it. Because the benefit leaks outward, markets under-provide field data and advisory services — exactly the goods with the highest food-security return. This is the strongest case for public investment in the agtech stack.

Precision changes the unit of policy. The old question of how much fertilizer the country needs gives way to which plot needs which nutrient, and whether the farmer can afford it this week. That is a data question before it is a subsidy question. Public soil maps, open weather data and extension networks buy a multiplier: every downstream input becomes cheaper to target.

The same logic runs through early warning. Famine is rarely a surprise; it is a forecast that never reached the people who needed to act. Satellite monitoring detects poor rainfall, failed planting and price spikes months before a food crisis peaks. What remains scarce is the habit of acting early, when the intervention is cheap.

Plot 05 · Climate

Climate Is Now a Production Variable

Heat, water and timing are now moving targets in every growing season.

Climate change has already reached the field. One clean estimate finds human-caused warming has slowed global agricultural productivity growth by about 21 per cent since 1961, with the heaviest losses in warmer regions — Africa, Latin America and the Caribbean, South Asia. The mechanism is simple: crops have thermal limits, water has a schedule, and both are now violated more often.

The IPCC is blunt: without adaptation, staple yields decline as warming proceeds, and the risk concentrates in the tropics. Adaptation is agronomy with a weather forecast attached — drought-tolerant varieties, short-season cultivars, supplementary irrigation for the critical weeks, index insurance that pays when a satellite says the rain failed.

Adaptation technology is the same stack, re-tuned for volatility: genetics that tolerate stress, water control that stretches a scarce supply, information that arrives before the stress, finance that absorbs the loss. Climate does not change the toolkit. It changes the urgency and which trait matters in every breeding decision and extension message.

The adaptation hedge is a yield strategy

A drought-tolerant variety that yields a little less in a good year but survives a bad one is not a compromise. Over ten seasons it is the difference between a farm and a household that has left farming. Its value is in the years that used to wipe people out.

A financing corollary follows. Smallholder adaptation is cheap relative to the crises it prevents, but it must be paid before the shock, on farms with no savings — the economic heart of food security in a warming world.

Plot 06 · The Smallholder

The Economics of a Half-Hectare Bet

Technology reaches smallholders only when the bet is redesigned.

Five hundred million farms under two hectares (about 85 per cent of all farms, on roughly 9 per cent of agricultural land) supply most of the food where hunger is deepest. That sentence explains why agricultural technology and food security so often pass without meeting: the technology is built for farms that can absorb a bad bet, and the food security problem lives on farms that cannot.

Consider the decision a smallholder faces. An improved seed costs more than last season's saved grain. It must be trusted before it has proven itself on this soil, in this rainfall. It must be learned while the household is short of labour and time. And if it fails, there is no insurance, no savings, no second season. The bettor carries the whole downside.

Economists describe this precisely: one farmer's adoption benefits neighbours, buyers and the public purse, while the costs stay private. Left to itself, the market under-adopts. The farmer pays the full price and captures part of the value; the community captures the rest for free. The result is what the yield-gap data shows — good technology on the shelf while the farms that need it cannot afford the risk.

The smallholder is not slow to adopt good technology. The smallholder is rationally avoiding a bet they cannot afford to lose. The adoption gap, in one sentence

The counter-moves are known. Break the upfront cost into instalments that match harvest cash flows. Guarantee the result, or refund the failure. Deliver through people already trusted — extension agents, input dealers, lead farmers, the neighbour whose field is the demonstration. Pay for the spillover with public money. Pay-as-you-go solar rewrote energy access with this playbook; it fits seed, irrigation, storage and insurance the same way.

Finance is the hinge. Smallholder credit fails for a structural reason: irregular income, no collateral, and covariate risk — when one farmer's crop fails, every farmer's failed, so no local lender can diversify. Index insurance and satellite-verified lending pay out on a measurable trigger, not a costly field visit. Where they work, adoption stops being heroism.

Plot 07 · The Agenda

From Technology to Food Security

Five levers, in the order in which they are cheap.

Everything above points to one conclusion: the world's food security problem is a delivery problem with a known solution set. Five levers move the system, and their order matters, because each one makes the next cheaper.

Figure 5 — The five levers, from shelf to soil

Schematic. Each lever raises the return to the next; none pays off without storage to protect the harvest and finance to protect the farmer. Delivery means moving the whole chain.

1 · Extension and digital advisory at scale

Public extension, once the main channel from agronomy to farm, was hollowed out for decades. Rebuilding it through digital channels (radio, phone, agents with soil and weather data) is the cheapest way to raise the skill of millions of farms at once. The unit of delivery is the timed, specific, trusted instruction.

2 · Finance that matches farm cash flows

Harvest incomes arrive once or twice a year; input costs arrive before planting. Credit, insurance and input subsidies must follow that calendar: pay-as-you-go for equipment, pre-approved credit that activates when the rains are forecast, index insurance on a satellite trigger. The goal is a bet the farmer can afford to lose.

3 · Storage, cold chains and roads

Infrastructure is the least glamorous lever and the most reliable. Every percentage point of post-harvest loss avoided is production that required no new land, water or seed. Storage lets farmers sell later, when prices recover, instead of all at once, when they collapse.

4 · Data and markets as public goods

Soil maps, open weather data, price information and early-warning systems produce value far beyond what any private buyer will pay. Fund them as public goods and let private services build on top. The public sector's job is the layer nobody can charge for.

5 · Measure adoption, not distribution

Programmes are still scored by inputs distributed: tonnes of seed, bags of fertilizer, apps downloaded. The field measures sustained use, seasons after the subsidy ends. A seed distributed and never replanted is a cost, not an outcome. Score delivery by what stays in the field.

The agenda is affordable because the yield gap is large. Small, durable gains on millions of small farms compound into regional food security; headline investments on model farms do not. The binding constraint was never calories per hectare at the frontier. It is technology per hectare at the back of the field.
